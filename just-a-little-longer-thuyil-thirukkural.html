“Just a Little Longer…” Thuyil — When Rest Begins to Take the Place of Life

  • 20th October 2026

“Neduneer maravi madi thuyil naangum
Keduneerar kaamak kalan.”

— Thirukkural 605

We have now travelled through three of the four quiet obstacles identified by

Thiruvalluvar.

The first voice said:

“I’ll do it later…”

That was Neduneer — postponement.

The second said:

“I’ll remember…”

That brought us to Maravi — forgetfulness.

The third said:

“I don’t feel like doing it now…”

That led us to Madi — inertia, the repeated preference for immediate comfort over

necessary effort.

Now Arun’s story brings us to the fourth voice.

Perhaps it is the gentlest-sounding voice of them all:

“Just a little longer…”

Arun Sets the Alarm

The deadline for the training programme is getting dangerously close.

Arun has already postponed contacting his teacher.

He has forgotten about the application.

He has opened the form and stepped away when he saw the effort involved.

But he has not completely given up.

He makes another plan.

“Tomorrow morning I’ll wake up early and finish it.”

He sets an alarm.

Morning comes.

The alarm rings.

For a moment, Arun remembers exactly why he set it.

The application.

The deadline.

The opportunity.

He reaches for his phone and switches off the alarm.

Then comes a familiar thought:

“Just another half an hour…”

He closes his eyes.

When he wakes again, much of the morning is gone.

The fourth ship has arrived.

Thuyil — துயில்.

But before going further, we need to make an important distinction.

Sleep Is Not the Enemy

Sleep is necessary.

Our bodies need it.

Our minds need it.

Adequate sleep supports our well-being and prepares us to participate fully in life.

So Thuyil should not be understood as saying:

“Sleep is bad.”

Nor should waking up early automatically be treated as a virtue for everyone.

The concern here is excessive sleep — sleep that goes beyond genuine restoration

and begins to consume time that would otherwise be available for living, learning,

working and growing.

A useful way to understand the distinction is:

**Rest should help us return to life.

It should not gradually take the place of life.**

“Five More Minutes”

The alarm rings.

“Five more minutes.”

Five becomes fifteen.

Fifteen becomes thirty.

The morning walk disappears.

Breakfast becomes rushed.

We leave late.

The beginning of the day becomes hurried.

And later we may say:

“I just don’t have enough time.”

Sometimes our shortage of time begins before the day has properly begun.

But this does not mean that every extra minute of sleep is a problem.

There are days when the body genuinely needs more rest.

There may be periods of intense work, travel or exhaustion when additional sleep is

entirely appropriate.

The question is not:

“Did I sleep longer today?”

The deeper question is:

“Is this rest restoring me — or has ‘a little

longer’ become an unconscious

pattern?”

When Rest Becomes Avoidance

Sometimes sleep is not only about tiredness.

It can also become a way of postponing our encounter with something

uncomfortable.

There is work waiting.

An examination.

An unfinished assignment.

A difficult responsibility.

An uncertain decision.

A conversation we do not want to face.

Remaining in bed a little longer temporarily postpones all of them.

For those few minutes, we do not have to begin.

So alongside the question:

“Am I tired?”

we may occasionally need another:

“Am I resting — or am I avoiding?”

The answer will not always be the same.

There is no need to judge ourselves whichever answer appears.

The purpose is simply to notice.

Sleep Is Not the Only Way We “Switch

Off”

Thiruvalluvar’s word here is Thuyil — sleep.

We should preserve that meaning.

But modern life gives us other ways of temporarily withdrawing from what we do not

want to face:

Endless scrolling.

Binge watching.

Random browsing.

Gaming.

Moving from one short video to another.

These are not literally what Thiruvalluvar means by Thuyil.

That distinction is important.

Yet sometimes they can perform a similar function:

They can become ways of escaping from something that

is waiting for our attention.

We pick up the phone for five minutes.

Forty minutes disappear.

We open one video.

Another begins.

Then another.

The task is still waiting.

The discomfort has temporarily disappeared.

But part of our time has disappeared with it.

The problem is not the phone.

It is not entertainment.

It is not recreation.

The useful question is:

“Is this something I consciously chose —

or something I entered because I did

not want to face what needed my

attention?”

The Four Ships Are Now Together

Let us return to Arun.

What began as a simple decision has gradually changed.

First:

“I’ll do it tomorrow.”

Neduneer.

Then:

“I must remember to message the teacher when I get home.”

But he forgot.

Maravi.

Later he opened the application.

It required effort.

“I don’t feel like doing this today.”

Madi.

Finally, he decided to wake early and complete it.

The alarm rang.

“Just a little longer…”

Thuyil.

Now something important becomes visible.

These four do not necessarily operate

separately.

One can create the conditions for another.

We postpone.

Time passes.

We forget.

When we remember again, the task may have become more urgent or more difficult.

Our mind resists the effort.

We look for comfort or escape.

And then the task is postponed again.

A cycle can form:

Neduneer → Maravi → Madi → Thuyil /

Escape → Neduneer again

Four apparently separate tendencies can become one repeating movement.

The Deadline Passes

In Arun’s case, the ten days eventually come to an end.

The application deadline passes.

He does not submit the form.

A few weeks later, one of his friends tells him some good news.

The friend had applied for the same training programme.

He has been selected.

Arun listens and says:

“I was also thinking of applying for that.”

There is something deeply revealing in that sentence.

Arun wanted the opportunity.

He thought about it.

He intended to act.

But:

Intending to do something and doing it

are not the same thing.

No one prevented Arun from applying.

There was no enemy working against him.

His ability had not disappeared.

He never consciously decided:

“I don’t want this opportunity.”

And yet the opportunity was lost.

Not through one enormous mistake.

But through a sequence of ordinary moments:

Later.

I’ll remember.

Not now.

Just a little longer.

When Small Choices Join Together

This is where Thiruvalluvar’s Kural begins to reveal another layer.

A single moment of postponement may seem trivial.

One forgotten task may seem harmless.

One moment of avoiding effort may seem insignificant.

A little extra sleep may appear to have no consequence.

And individually, that may often be true.

But when these tendencies begin to support one another, they can create a pattern.

And that pattern can repeat without our consciously deciding to create it.

So perhaps the important question is not merely:

“Did I do this once?”

It is:

“Is there a recurring movement here?”

Four Questions for Self-Reflection

Across the previous articles, we have already asked questions about postponement,

forgetfulness and immediate comfort.

Now that all four tendencies are visible, let us look at the larger pattern.

These questions are not a quiz.

They are mirrors.

10.

What is one opportunity I have already

lost because I delayed too long?

It need not be something dramatic.

A course.

An application.

A conversation.

A relationship.

A health decision.

A chance to learn.

A chance to begin.

Do not use the memory to punish yourself.

Use it to understand.

11.

Of these four — Neduneer, Maravi, Madi

and excessive Thuyil — which affects

me most?

Which voice appears most often?

“Later…”

“I’ll remember…”

“I don’t feel like it now…”

“Just a little longer…”

Simply recognise it.

Recognition itself matters.

12.

If I continue living in the same way for

another five years, where will this ship

take me?

Do not rush past this question.

Stay with it for a while.

Think about the area of life where this tendency operates most strongly.

Your studies.

Your work.

Your health.

Your relationships.

Your dreams.

Your growth.

If nothing changes for five years:

Where might you arrive?

13.

What is one small but meaningful action I

can take today — not tomorrow — to

change that direction?

Not a complete transformation.

Not ten resolutions.

One action.

Small enough to do.

Meaningful enough to matter.

What is it?

Rest Consciously

For each of the four tendencies, we have discovered a simple response.

Neduneer → Begin.

Maravi → Record it.

Madi → Take the next small action.

And now:

Excessive Thuyil → Rest consciously.

This does not mean fighting our genuine need for sleep.

It means bringing awareness into our relationship with rest.

When the voice says:

“Just a little longer…”

we can ask:

“Is this genuine rest — or avoidance?”

Sometimes the honest answer will be:

“I really do need more rest.”

Then rest.

At another time, we may recognise:

“No. I am rested enough. I simply do not want to face what comes next.”

That moment of recognition matters.

Because we are no longer moving entirely on autopilot.

There is now a possibility of choice.

Why Did Thiruvalluvar Call Them a Ship?

We have now met all four:

Neduneer.

Maravi.

Madi.

Thuyil.

But one important part of the Kural still remains.

Thiruvalluvar does not merely list four undesirable tendencies.

He calls them:

A “kalam” — a ship.

Once we board a ship, we do not have to keep walking for the journey to continue.

We may sit.

We may become distracted.

We may even sleep.

But the ship continues to move.

And while we are occupied with something else, it carries us in a direction.

Could our repeated habits work in the same way?

Could small choices be carrying our lives even when we are not consciously

choosing where to go?

And if every ship has a direction, another question eventually becomes unavoidable:

Where is this ship taking me?

We have now identified the four ships.

In the final article, we will look beyond them—

towards their direction, their destination, and perhaps the most important question

of this entire journey:

Where do I actually want to go?
